## PHASE 0 — The Mindset: Pick a Game You'll Play Forever

**The story:** You're on one of two paths. Before any tactic, you need the right operating system, and most people quit at exactly the point where the real learning starts.

### 0.1 The two paths
- **Path 1, the forgettable brand:** you post randomly and hope something sticks. People don't know who you are or what you do, so they don't engage, and they definitely don't buy.
- **Path 2, the intentional brand:** you own what people associate with you, and your content builds trust and moves people toward a decision.
- If you're already on Path 2, you've probably hit a ceiling. This masterclass is about breaking through it.

### 0.2 Pick a game you're willing to die on the field for
- The whole point is to find a game you get to play forever. Retiring at 40 to sit on a beach isn't the goal.
- You don't have to work 12 hours a day forever, but productive growth has no end.
- Once you've picked your game and you're willing to die on the field, winning is inevitable.

### 0.3 Train like a personal trainer would program you
- If you walk into the gym and start on Ronnie Coleman's routine, you'll get injured and quit. **The best program is the one you'll stick with**, because results come from duration.
- Content works the same way. Don't copy MrBeast's production or Gary Vee's volume on day one. Aspire to it, but start with what you can sustain for years.
- Build the *habit* first. Caleb's advice to new lifters: "Go to the gym for 10 minutes, 5 days a week." Driving to the gym is the hard part, and posting is the content equivalent.

### 0.4 Volume negates luck
- Once you know the strategy, it's commoditized. This masterclass gives you the whole strategy, and that still isn't the edge.
- **The edge is iteration distance.** You make one rep, then iterate off it on the second rep. How far each iteration moves you is where all the value is.
- It's about doing it a huge number of times and improving at increasing rates.

### 0.5 The fork in the road (reps 10–30)
- After 10 to 30 reps you think "I should be good by now," and you still suck.
- That's when you realize it might take about 500 reps. **Most people quit here.** If you've posted consistently for even 2–3 months, you're already ahead of most people who start.

### 0.6 The core skill: self-diagnosis (A vs. B)
- Looking at a piece and saying "that looks cool" is taste.
- The real skill is looking at *your own* piece and seeing exactly where it falls short. For example: "My hook was too slow and the visual didn't grab me. The winner's did."
- Break a made rep into pieces, compare each piece to the best in your category, and apply what you learn to the next rep.
- **Why a coach or expert helps:** alone, you have too many unknown unknowns. Someone who knows the craft can point out the gaps you can't see.

### 0.7 Everyone is bad at the beginning, so leave your ego out of it
- There are about 100 things you know you're bad at and another 100 you don't know about yet. Nobody is born knowing "the 3 hooks."
- The people who get good close several gaps per rep, stay egoless about the learning, and don't stop.
- Kane's early content was monotone and slow, with weak topics, and he looked like he didn't want to be there.
- Caleb admitted on camera that he thought he "looked fat" in his own course footage, and posted it anyway. **Your inner critic isn't propelling you toward your goals. The results are.** If the content is getting traction, nobody else is thinking about what you're insecure about.

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Decide which path you're on today (forgettable or intentional).
- [ ] Write down the game you'd play for 10+ years.
- [ ] Choose a starting routine you can *actually* sustain, then commit to 100 reps before you judge yourself.
- [ ] After every rep, write one A-vs-B comparison against a winner in your niche.

## PHASE 1 — Strategy: Pick Your Game Before You Make Content

**The story:** Most gurus hand out the same advice to everyone. The right strategy depends on what you're selling, so you pick the game first and the tactics follow from it.

### 1.1 The 5 Content Games
| # | Game | What it is | Goal |
|---|------|-----------|------|
| 1 | **Entertainment media** | MrBeast, streamers, anything that isn't tactically usable | Maximum views (CPM, creator funds, brand deals) |
| 2 | **Education media** | Content you can apply to fix a problem | Maximum views |
| 3 | **Commoditized consumer product** | Couches, hats, clothing (needs no explanation) | Awareness: show it, it's clear |
| 4 | **Consumer product that needs education** | AG1 ("green powder in a box") | Awareness plus a quick education gap |
| 5 | **B2B** | Services, coaching, info products, B2B SaaS | Deep trust through lots of education |

- Nuance: Kane's tech/AI videos are *entertainment*, even though the topic is technical, because you can't walk away and apply them.
- **Blend education and entertainment** for an asymmetric outcome.

### 1.2 The media game is a losing game in 2025–2026
- It's arbing to zero, and AI content will run it over.
- Example: a creator with 7M views a month who still lives in his mom's basement. After paying his team he nets very little.
- You'd be competing against MrBeast, Netflix and the NFL. You'll lose.
- If you do play media, play the **brand game**: a narrow ICP that's audience-matched lets you charge premium sponsorship rates.

### 1.3 All business = Traffic → Funnels → Offers
- **Path A:** start with an offer, then go get traffic.
- **Path B (the common one, and Kane's):** start with traffic and figure out the offer later.
- **The trap in Path B:**
  1. You get addicted to views, broaden the topics, and widen the audience.
  2. Revenue caps out and decays as competition and AI rise.
  3. You try to sell an offer and the broad audience doesn't want it. Polling them gives you answers all over the place.
  4. Either you stay stuck in media, or you **rebuild a second, narrow audience from scratch** around the offer. That's what happened to Kane.
- Caleb's version of the same trap: his 68k Instagram followers came from landscape photography 10 years ago. They aren't his buyers, so he has to convert or lose most of them.

### 1.4 Creator vs. entrepreneur using content
- There are two different buckets. Pick yours honestly.
  - **Creator/influencer:** fame, parties, exposure. That's the media game, and breaking seven figures there is rare (top 1% or less).
  - **Entrepreneur in the trenches:** uses organic content as a cheat-code lever for a business. Kane is here, and so is Caleb: "My goal is purely to become known with my potential customers."
- **Escape velocity (the Joe Rogan exception):** once you're huge you can cover anything, but that's the media game. To win early, go narrow.
- Brands pay because your audience matches their buyer. **The wider you go, the less certain brands are, and the less they pay.** Going wider works against dollars.
- Narrow is slower, though. It's an ego game.

### 1.5 The field is wide open
- Very few creators can hold a real business conversation.
- The cost of building and the cost of attention are both heading to zero.
- A data-driven, science-based approach beats people playing on gut feel. It's open season in every lane, even though it doesn't look that way from outside.

### 1.6 The master law: Content Minutes (Kane's most important framework)
- Trust, and buying, require a certain number of minutes watched. **1 minute = 1 block.** A block only counts if it's actually watched.
  - Email lead magnet ≈ 4 blocks · $97 product ≈ 12 blocks · $10k product ≈ 400 blocks
- **Bingeability** stacks blocks fast. If a $97 product needs 8 blocks and you get 8 watched in one session, they buy.
- **Short vs. long math:** a 25-minute YouTube video gets roughly 8–10 minutes of average view duration (about 10 blocks). A 60-second short gets about 30 seconds (half a block). **You need 20 shorts to equal one long-form video in trust.**
- YouTube is also *intent-based*: people chose to watch it, rather than having it fed to them.
- Caleb frames the same law with the **Harley dealership sunk-cost effect**: the finance team runs your credit in seconds but keeps you there for 15 minutes and walks you through the whole store. The more time you invest with someone, the more willing you are to act on their offer. **Short form drives awareness. Long form is where conversion happens.**

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Circle your game (1–5).
- [ ] Decide: offer-first or traffic-first? If traffic-first, sketch your offer *now* to avoid the trap.
- [ ] Estimate your block ladder: blocks to lead, blocks to low ticket, blocks to high ticket.

## PHASE 2 — Brand Foundation: Define Who You Are

**The story:** Once you know the game, you decide what you want people to think when they hear your name. Your brand exists whether you define it or not, and if you don't define it, your audience will do it for you.

### 2.1 What brand and branding actually are
- **Branding** = a pairing of things.
- **Good branding** = *an intentional pairing of relevant things, done consistently.*
- **Brand** = the byproduct: when the audience *inherently associates* those things together.
- Examples:
  - **Nike + Michael Jordan** → athletic greatness in basketball.
  - **Gary Vee + content** → he wrote *Crush It* around 2009 ("people will make money talking about the Smurfs on YouTube") and repeated the message for 15+ years.
  - **Harley-Davidson + freedom** → the open road, tearing off the suit.
- This makes branding *operational*: to be associated with X, deliberately pair yourself with X, over and over.

### 2.2 The Brand Journey Framework (reverse-engineer from the end)
1. **What's my desired outcome?**
2. **What do I have to be known for** for that to happen?
3. **What do I have to do** to be known for that? (Brand is actions, not just words.)
4. **What do I have to learn** to do that? ← This is your day-one to-do list.
- Most people posting content have no idea what they're trying to accomplish.
- The Cheshire Cat in *Alice in Wonderland*: "Where are you trying to end up?" "I don't know." "Then either way will take you there."
- This mirrors Kane's advice to **build a real skill before building an audience** (Phase 7.2). Step 4 is where that skill-building happens.

### 2.3 Choose your associations
- What do you want people to think when they hear your name? Business? Trustworthiness? Mental well-being?
- **Everything you create should reinforce those associations.**
- A meta example: Caleb's free course is itself a deliberate pairing of his firm with value, scaling brands, understanding attention, and trust.

### 2.4 Choose what you will NOT be associated with (the part most people ignore)
- In a saturated market you stand out partly by what you refuse. This is how you protect the brand from being diluted or misread.
- "Intentional" doesn't mean "good." You can intentionally pair yourself with the wrong things. If you keep appearing on podcasts or collabs with untrustworthy, snake-oil creators, your audience will associate you with them.
- **Awareness isn't worth the trade.** Turn down the biggest podcast if the host's brand damages trust. People would only become aware of you *in light of that association*.
- If you get a bad feeling in your gut, it's not worth the exposure.

### 2.5 The Brand Story Framework (Catalyst → Core Truth → Proof)
Forget the rigid "problem → transformation → outcome" template. It assumes a dramatic "aha" moment, a founder-only story, and a brand that never evolves. Great brands are built on consistency, distinction, and connecting with the right audience over time.
1. **Catalyst: why your brand exists.** What needs to change? What do you see that others don't? Why do you feel the need to act? (Phil Knight likely saw unmet demand, not just a way to get rich.)
2. **Core Truth: what makes you different.** A core conviction you share publicly. This isn't controversy for its own sake. Example: the musician Russ preaches artist independence and ownership in his music and in interviews.
   - Ask: What do I believe that others don't? What about my personality stands out? What do people compliment me on? Why would the audience care?
3. **Proof: how you reinforce it over and over.** A brand is what you *do* and prove, not what you say. It's an ongoing pattern of credibility. Example: Gary Vee proves "volume" with 15 years of practice, plus VaynerMedia using high-volume social tests to pick Super Bowl creative.
   - Ask: Does every piece reinforce my associations? What case studies establish credibility? What's the first thing people think when they hear my name?
- Caleb's own story: **Catalyst:** brands chase views but fail to build trust, because they lack big-picture clarity. **Core truth:** a strong brand comes from intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently, not from virality. **Proof:** he has scaled brands to millions in revenue, and he teaches it for free.

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Answer the 4 Brand Journey questions, and turn question 4 into this month's learning plan.
- [ ] List 3 associations you want and 3 you refuse.
- [ ] Write your Catalyst, Core Truth and Proof in one sentence each.

## PHASE 3 — Positioning, Topics & the Algorithm: Who Are You For?

**The story:** With the brand defined, you have to be *legible*: to the algorithm, to viewers, and to their memory. Then you find the gap nobody else fills.

### 3.1 The three hills Kane would die on
1. **Content minutes** (Phase 1.6)
2. **Audience matching**
3. **"Anti-views"**: on-target views beat total views

### 3.2 Find the gap and own it
- Your goal isn't to blend in. Find what's missing in your industry and own it. The gap might be information, a philosophy, or a personality that reaches people other creators can't.
- Ask: **What are people in my space saying that I disagree with? How can I bring a fresh perspective to what's been taught for years?**
- Example: if everyone preaches virality, lean into building long-term trust. When the algorithm changes, a views-only creator disappears, but a trusted brand gets *searched for*.

### 3.3 Your story is your secret weapon
- Nobody has your come-up story. Present all your beliefs through that lens.
- **The pivotal-moments exercise:** list the challenges you've overcome, turning points (getting laid off and landing something better), and experiences that shaped your view (for Caleb, working for Gary Vee).
- **Show, don't tell.** "I'm a hard worker" means nothing. The story of editing Gary's Trash Talk series 12–14 hours a day and then working about 32 hours straight from Thursday to Friday proves it. Jeff Bezos using a door as a desk says "resourceful" better than the word does.

### 3.4 Own a term
- People have room for about 200–300 names in their heads, like Dunbar's number for the internet.
- Saying a term wipes that list and brings up one person: "ManyChat" → Ganon. Hormozi owns "business." Gary Vee owns "attention."
- **The fastest route to cult-level category fandom is attaching yourself to a term.** Niche terms nobody owns yet are the easiest to claim.

### 3.5 Picking topics: start narrow, then expand in layers
- **Start narrow.** Early trust comes from being great at one thing and talking about it constantly. The friend who claims to be great at everything is trusted about nothing. The friend obsessed with woodworking is trusted about woodworking.
- **Gary Vee's path:** years of Wine Library TV (wine only) → South by Southwest marketing talks → *Crush It* → social media. His topics grew as his proven experience grew.
- **When to expand:**
  - You're genuinely world-class at the new topic, so don't talk about what you don't know.
  - You're known for your core topic and get consistent engagement on it.
  - Your audience keeps asking about something you've only hinted at.
- **Expand into adjacent topics, in layers, not leaps.** Brand strategy → breathwork is too big a jump. Inch toward it.
- **Never lose your roots:** Amazon went from books to everything, then reinforced its roots with Audible. Crayola brought back 8 retired colors (February 2025) as a nostalgia play. The band Knocked Loose still plays its early tracks for day-one fans. **Nostalgia wins**, so consider returning to an old format or sound.
- **These are guidelines, not rules.** If you want to make a video about your Harley, do it. It rounds you out.
- **Three topic questions:**
  1. What am I genuinely passionate about discussing? (Passion reads as conviction, and conviction drives action.)
  2. What will resonate with my audience *right now*? (For Caleb's audience, organic content is a black box and paid media isn't.)
  3. **What will change my audience's actions the most?** (This is the most important of the three.)

### 3.6 The 80/20 rule and interest stacking
- **80%** on your core topic, ideally tied to your offer. **20%** on your other interests, to be human.
- **Interest stacking:** coworkers go from "we both work here" to "we both love Kendrick" to friends. Every extra interest gives your audience another way to connect with you.
- Joe Rogan covers comedy, MMA, hunting, human optimization, conspiracies and politics. Share one interest with him and you might listen. Share three or four and you're a superfan.

> **⚖️ Reconcile — "one topic until retirement" vs. 80/20:** Kane's rule is about what the *algorithm* needs to classify you and match audiences. Caleb's is about what *humans* need to connect. Hold both: 80% of your output (and nearly all of your early output) stays in one topic, and the 20% is personality seasoning, not a second channel.

### 3.7 How the algorithm actually works
- Its only job is to keep people on the platform, because more watching means more ads, which means more money.
- To do that it has to know exactly what kind of content your channel makes, so it can serve it to the right people.
- **Transcription = a context map.** The platform transcribes your video in real time. Your video is essentially a long-tail blog post, so saying niche terms helps it classify you, like SEO. Hashtags and keywords are icing on top.
- **Sample pools:**
  1. You post. It's sent to about 200 people (a mix of subscribers and new viewers) who seem likely to like it.
  2. If they watch or like it more than usual, it goes to about 2,000 similar people, then keeps expanding in concentric circles.
  3. If the response is neutral, it might try another pool. If it's bad, it throttles fast. That's why bad videos die at about 200 views.
  4. As the circles widen, the audience gets "muddier" until the video runs out of people who like it.
- **Implication:** topic-hopping (tech → science → basketball) builds a mixed follower base, so every sample pool sends back a weak signal and nothing gets boosted.
- Media players can go *wider inside one topic* (paid marketing, viral marketing, and so on, all within "marketing"), but never jump categories (marketing → NBA).
- In an ideal world you'd run separate channels per niche. Kane says he'd have 17 tech channels if he had the time.

### 3.8 The virality myth, and small correct audiences
- You don't need virality. Chasing it means broader, more sensational topics.
- **What actually pays is dollars per view, on target.** Depth in a narrow slice creates category-level fandom.
- **A small, correct, engaged audience beats a massive indifferent one.** Some people with fewer than 10k followers make millions a year, because those 5,000 followers are their buyers. Some people with millions of followers make very little.
- In short form you can win either way (views game or niche game). On YouTube you don't want broad virality.

### 3.9 Followers are a credibility proxy
- The viewer's psychology: they see the video, judge it, look at the likes, comments, shares and saves, then check your profile's follower count.
- If you're below the **minimum credibility line**, they don't trust you. Above it, they do. There's no real difference between 180k and 365k followers.
- Rough thresholds: X/Twitter, a few thousand · Instagram, over 10k (the next step is about 100k) · YouTube, about 10k subscribers.
- Followers matter a lot for **brand deals**. Brands price on average views over your last 10 videos, engagement, and followers.
- The blue check used to signal this. Now it can be bought. Fake followers without engagement may fool casual viewers, but they won't fool marketers or high-ticket buyers.

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Write the gap you'll fill in one sentence ("Everyone says X; I say Y").
- [ ] List 5 pivotal moments from your story.
- [ ] Write your core topic in 5 words or fewer, plus your 20% interests.
- [ ] Choose the term you want to own.
- [ ] Audit your last 10 posts: how many are on topic?

## PHASE 4 — Mediums, Platforms & Cadence: Where and How Often

**The story:** Before you make anything, you choose the *form* you'll lead with, the *places* you'll post, and a *rhythm* you can keep. Play to your strengths so you can do this for years.

### 4.1 Choose your 80% medium
You'll eventually do all four. Pick the one you'll lean on most:
| Medium | Where it lives | Best for people who… | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Written** | LinkedIn, X, Facebook, email, newsletters, articles, ebooks | Can compress dense ideas into high value per sentence | Do I already journal? Does it make sense *without* me explaining it out loud? Do I enjoy rewriting 10 times? |
| **Video** | YouTube (long and short), TikTok, Reels, LinkedIn video, Facebook, Spotify video | Are comfortable on camera, or willing to improve in public | Can I handle criticism? Do visuals excite me? Can I speak concisely? |
| **Audio** | Podcasts | Want video's benefits without staring into the lens | Can I paint pictures with words alone? Do I enjoy deep dives? Does my voice hold attention? |
| **Graphic/visual** | Carousels, infographics, PDF carousels on LinkedIn | Like explaining ideas visually (Canva, Figma, Adobe) | Can I simplify complex ideas into one image? Do I enjoy polishing? |

- **Video is the highest-leverage medium:** one shoot becomes long form, shorts, podcast audio, quote graphics, LinkedIn posts, newsletter copy, and stills.
- **"Bunny brain":** experts ramble down rabbit trails. That's not a reason to skip video. It's a reason to get a better editor.
- **Audio as a stepping stone:** if video terrifies you, start a podcast. It often proves video isn't that scary.
- **A monotone voice can be your bit.** Whatever you think is a weakness, turn it into an advantage. Some of the best podcasters and comedians are monotone and sarcastic.
- **LinkedIn (as of early 2025)** is maturing like Facebook did around 2013–14. Comments show your post to the commenter's whole network, so it has true virality. Written posts are especially strong there.
- **Carousels are strong on Instagram.** Keep them simple. Visualize Value is the model. Hand-drawn or photographed *physical* objects (a post-it, a printed quote) also perform well.

### 4.2 Choose 2–3 platforms
- **Avoid single-platform risk.** In January 2025 TikTok went dark in the US for 12 hours, and creators who lived only there panicked. Algorithms also change constantly and roll out differently to different accounts.
- Don't try to post everywhere. Go deep on 2–3 with *original, platform-specific* content.
- **How to pick:**
  1. Start where you're already comfortable. Build the habit, not your comfort zone.
  2. Where do you already spend your time? Turn doom-scrolling into study.
  3. Where is your audience? Form a hypothesis from your Brand Journey, then test it. Are leads actually coming in? If not, either the content sucks or the platform is wrong.
  4. Do they want quick hits (TikTok), deep dives (YouTube), or thought leadership (LinkedIn)?
- Caleb started on LinkedIn and Instagram because he believed his clients were on LinkedIn, then expanded to YouTube and a podcast.
- **The Eye of Sauron approach:** put most of your focus on one platform while the others run in maintenance mode, then rotate.

### 4.3 Double down on what works, and track conversions over vanity
- When one medium or platform is popping, pour gas on it. That's not the moment to level up weaker skills. Don't kill the weaker one, just reduce its share of your effort.
- **Track conversions off-platform,** not just on-platform performance: streams, hoodie sales, editing gigs, inbound investment deals, email signups. Many creators waste 1–3 years optimizing for a vanity metric.
- It's both: platform performance gets you seen, and conversion pays the bills. Caleb would take 30–40% fewer likes for a much higher conversion rate.

### 4.4 Quality vs. quantity: the Accordion Method
- **You don't know what quality is. Your audience decides.** Some of the most viral videos ever are grainy phone clips shot at night.
- **Volume is a tool for learning faster.** Ten posts in a week teach you more than ten posts in a month.
- **Then compress the accordion:** once you know what your audience calls quality, put more effort into each piece.
- **Start from your highest-leverage platform:** the one driving the most results, and the one where a single piece can be repurposed into many (usually long form).
- **Look at conversion *rate*, not just absolute leads.** On one team, YouTube produced the most leads, but LinkedIn converted at the highest *percentage* by a mile. Scaling LinkedIn's reach made it the top channel. At one point they spent $20–30k a month on LinkedIn organic alone.
- On a previous team, Caleb went from 3 to 5 YouTube videos a week. They were barely trimmed, just to learn what the audience wanted.
- **Perfect is the enemy of posted.** Sirens and semi-trucks interrupted Caleb's course shoot, and he posted anyway, then noted fixes for the next one. If you can only manage one post every other week, start there.
- Caleb can post less often from day one only because 2,000+ business-owner Q&As already told him what his audience wants. Without that data, start with volume.

> **⚖️ Reconcile — Kane "volume negates luck" vs. Caleb "more effort per piece":** they describe two stages of the same accordion. Early on, high volume and one-at-a-time iteration buy you data (Kane's reps, Caleb's learning phase). Once you know what your audience calls quality, compress: fewer, bigger pieces (Caleb's current stance, and Kane's batched YouTube with an A-team).

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Pick your 80% medium and your 2–3 platforms, and name the one you'll focus on first.
- [ ] Define what a "conversion" means for you, and start tracking it.
- [ ] Set a posting cadence you can hold for 90 days.

## PHASE 5 — Short-Form Craft: Building One Great Video

**The story:** Now you make the thing. You start with the Lego framework, then go brick by brick: the hook, the script, the visuals, the edit, the format, and the cover.

### 5.1 The Lego Brick Framework (the core model)
Kane's worldview: break anything complex down to atomic units, find the options for each unit, and stack them.

**The 7 bricks of a short-form video:**
1. **Topic**: what it's about
2. **Angle**: what's interesting about it
3. **Hook structure**: visual, text and spoken hook
4. **Story structure / video type**: case study, breakdown, listicle, narrative, and so on
5. **Visual format**: split screen, full screen, circle overlay, in-the-world, vlog POV
6. **Key visuals**: A-roll, B-roll, graphics
7. **Audio**: music and sound effects

**The process:** find the winning creators and videos in your category, explode their best videos into the 7 bricks, hold the world-class bricks constant, improve the weak ones, restack them into your own formation, and post.

- **Beginners:** change topic and angle each video. Hold hook, story and visual format constant and copy what's working. This isn't copying in a lazy sense. It shrinks the number of unknowns you're juggling.
- **Advanced:** invent new formats, or borrow from other industries and remix.
- **The contrast play:** use winners for 6 of the 7 bricks and be first-mover on one.

### 5.2 The Hook: three layers that must align
- **Visual hook** (what's on screen), **text hook** (title text, captions), **spoken hook** (what's said).
- **Misalignment → confusion → churn.** The opposite of confusion is comprehension.
- **The time you get:** about 2–3 seconds on short form. On YouTube long form, the industry says 30 seconds, but Caleb says 15–20 seconds max. In that window viewers decide: is this what I clicked on, and is this the right person to hear it from?

**The three jobs of a hook, in order:**
1. **Visual stop (the "stun gun" / thumb-stopper).** Light is faster than sound, so the viewer sees before they read or hear. Be *different* from the feed. The generic podcast set (wood slats plus a plant) doesn't stand out anymore. A distinctive set does.
2. **Context in the first sentence.** The brain learns top-down: context → how → examples → lesson. "Jump was crazy" is meaningless because it leaves out who jumped, and over what. All three layers can carry context: an arrow on screen, the spoken line, the text.
3. **Contrast.** The gap between what they expect and what you show them. That gap creates curiosity.
   - **Direct contrast:** state the common belief, then flip it ("Most people think X… but actually…"). "But" is the contrast word.
   - **Implied contrast:** a bare contrarian statement ("Cars are driving backwards and nobody knows why"). The viewer has to fill in the belief themselves, which is weaker.
   - **The −100 to +100 spectrum:** implied contrast moves them from 0 to −100 (a 100-point arc). Direct contrast leans them to +100, then flips them to −100 (a 200-point arc), which is more violent.
   - Caleb's example: "Subscribers, views and likes don't matter. What actually matters is…"
- **The pattern interrupt:** Gary Vee's "blueberry clip" opens with him chomping blueberries: "I eat blueberries like it's my job… you missed the first 30 I ate." It was his most viral clip at the time.
- Unexpected-fact hooks are getting tired. Thought-provoking questions still work.
- **Speed to value:** get to the value, or tease it, as fast as possible.

### 5.3 The spoken layer
- Use as few words as possible, in active voice ("the dog jumped," not "the jump was made by the dog").
- **The KPI is absorption rate.** Does the viewer understand 100% of what you said, with only one possible meaning?
- Cut fluff, but add words when clarity needs them. Over-cutting drops absorption below 100%.
- **Delivery:** vary your cadence. Speed up then slow down, get louder then quieter. The change is a pattern interrupt that signals emphasis.

### 5.4 The text layer
- **Captions:** the exact spoken words, one or two at a time, styled.
- **Title text:** bigger words that aren't spoken verbatim ("Stop making your hooks like this"). They provide context and a curiosity gap.
- **Corroboration:** the viewer sees a visual, guesses what it means, then uses the text to confirm the guess. For example: a robot with tendons, plus "first robot with human tendons," plus the text "Artificial robot has human tendons." That triple alignment makes something click in the brain, and the viewer locks in.
- Skipping title text is allowed, but it removes one way to triangulate, so your words and visuals have to be even tighter.

### 5.5 Visuals
- **High motion, high color, high contrast.**
- Why motion: a deer grazing looks up when it senses motion. The brain is wired for changes in motion. Most feeds are static (bedroom, car), so motion locks people in.
- Podcast clips are hard to make viral because they have little motion. Add artificial motion: push-ins, lens flares.
- **Interesting subjects:** if you're attractive, play into it. With B-roll, pick visually interesting material.
- **Niche maturity sets the bar.** Tech and AI is a mature niche, so visuals must be very tight. Dentistry is immature, so even a grainy overhead root-canal shot can pop.

### 5.6 Be human, not a robot
- Bring your whole personality on camera. The lens is weird, but monotone info-dumping makes you forgettable.
- **Share losses, not just wins.** The friend who's always winning gets annoying and isn't believable. Losses are more relatable, and people learn more from them.
- Caleb's client example: the audience thought she was a cold "information robot," but in real life she was hilarious. People who met her said, "You're so much nicer than I expected." Showing her human side fixed that.
- Caleb's own first YouTube video told how a major client cut his pay in half and he had to fire friends he'd hired. Transparency builds trust faster than posturing.
- **Double down on what people compliment.** About 90% of Caleb's client calls end with someone praising his energy, so he brings that energy, and his side-comment habit, into his content.

### 5.7 Scripting: the question-chain method
- The first line (context) should open **one clear question** in the viewer's head. If it opens several questions of equal strength, you have a problem.
- Answer that question in the next line, which opens the next question. Repeat.
- Example: "Google dropped Gemini 3 and it's crazy" → *what can it do?* → "It can do X" → *how do I use it?* → "Go here" → *what does it cost?* → "Here's a discount."
- Viewers are constantly guessing what comes next. You either confirm the guess or add contrast. It's a zigzag.
- **Macro structure:** roughly 3 story sections: intro (the "holy—what is this?"), how it works, and the implication.
- **Answer objections inside the script.** Viewers of educational content are constantly thinking "Why should I trust this? Will it work for *me*? In my industry?" Mine your comments for recurring skepticism and answer it in the next video, the way a good VSL answers objections before the sales call. This removes friction and builds credibility.

### 5.8 The Two-Dopamine-Hit Rule
- Two hits of dopamine before they would churn means they're locked in. One hit means maybe. Zero means they bounce.
- For education, a dopamine hit is something non-obvious that they can use to solve a problem. (Example from the episode: the DM-cover insight in 5.11.)

### 5.9 Editing
- **The most common mistakes:** visuals misaligned with the words, and pacing that's off.
- **The eyes-closed test:** listen without watching. If you're bored, it's too slow, so cut tighter. If you can't absorb it, it's too fast, so add breathing room.
- **Visual selection:** ask what the best way to show *this sentence* is. Sometimes it's no visual, just you on screen.
- **The comprehension decay graph:** comprehension starts at 100% and every word or frame can only hold it or lower it. Once it drops far enough, they bounce. People churn because they're *unclear or uninterested*. Make the first 5–10 seconds airtight. If someone leaves while clear on what the video is about, fine. It wasn't for them.
- Retention always slopes down. Look for **abnormal cliffs**, which usually mean confusion or visual misalignment.

### 5.10 What formats are winning now (as of January 2026)
- Most formats can work in most niches.
- **The hottest format: narrative-driven visual storytelling.** Example: Chloe Shu's "30 lessons in 30 days by age 30." The structure is: "I had this problem → tried this → it failed → did this → here's what worked." It's a personal story, "take it or leave it," not "you should do these 3 things."
- **Format seasons** follow platform maturity:
  1. **About 2020–22 (TikTok, then Reels launch):** transactional tutorials and listicles ("5 best AI tools"). No supply, so they won. Now they're saturated and easy for AI to produce.
  2. **Case study breakdowns:** "this person got X result, here's how." Still a how-to, but with more original context.
  3. **Now:** personal narrative, which is **one-of-one**. Lived experiences can't be copied. (The transactional playbook was open-sourced by Kane, Orin, Alex Garcia and others, which flooded supply.)
- The one-of-one example: Marshall Haas (sold his offshore staffing company) posting "the 8 cars I bought after selling." Few people can make that video, so even simpler editing cuts through.
- **If 50 other people could make your video, you need to be the best of the 50, or already have a brand.**
- Kane's live experiment: taking over marketing short form in 2026 from zero (@callawaymarketing on Instagram). He has a deep brand on one platform and almost none on another.
- The host's plan, "40 lessons before 40," uses stories like the Rick Ross DM and a visit to Evander Holyfield's old estate, plus vault footage.

### 5.11 Series, numbering and covers (bingeability)
- **Numbered series** ("Day 4 of 10") exploit the puzzle-completing brain. Watching day 4 makes you want days 1–3. That's forced connective tissue.
- The con: if you commit to daily and burn out, you break the promise. **Stack episodes ahead.** The same goes for a podcast: have about 5 banked.
- The Chloe Shu formula = numbered series + personal narrative + strong visual execution.
- **Covers ("thumbnails" for short form):** when someone binges your profile, covers tell them what each video is about, which drives bingeability and content minutes.
- **The hidden DM insight:** virality comes from shares (the K-factor: 100 see it, 3 share it, 5 more see it, and so on), and most shares happen in DMs and group chats. **In a DM the video doesn't loop. Only the cover shows.** A better cover means more clicks (3 out of 5 people instead of 2). The host's example: his wife sends 6 memes and he picks 2 based on the cover alone.

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Pick 3 winning videos in your niche and explode each into the 7 bricks.
- [ ] Write a hook: visual stop + context sentence + direct contrast (or a pattern interrupt).
- [ ] Script using the question chain, answer one common objection, and land 2 dopamine hits before 50% of runtime.
- [ ] Run the eyes-closed test before posting.
- [ ] Design covers for your profile grid and for the DM view.

## PHASE 6 — Iteration & Analytics: Getting Better Every Rep

**The story:** One good video isn't the goal. A system that improves every rep is.

### 6.1 Don't batch as a beginner
- Batching stretches the iteration loop. You repeat the same mistake 5 times before any feedback comes back.
- **Beginner:** make one, post it, watch it do badly, explode it with the Lego framework, fix one or a few things, and repeat.
- The topic checklist: is it relevant to the audience you want? Is it interesting? How is it framed?
- **Advanced:** once you know the fundamentals, batch and systematize for volume. Kane went one at a time for about 300 reps, and now does 2–3 at a time, working toward 5–10.

> **⚖️ Reconcile — batching:** Caleb notes that busy founders often *must* batch (a full Thursday or Friday of filming). That's fine once you have fundamentals. Just watch for the pressure of a big batch day, which can cause anxiety and weak performances, and build a supportive filming environment (Phase 11.4).

### 6.2 Reading retention
- Kane barely uses charts. He reviews **sentence by sentence**: "Am I clear on what this sentence means and what I should take away from it?" If not, there's drop-off.
- The YouTube Shorts benchmark: **about 70%+ still watching at 3 seconds means a banger. Below 50% is a big problem.**
- **Data is confirmatory and lagging.** The leading indicator is following the principles *while making* the video.

### 6.3 Study your top 10%, not your average
- Every month, take the last 30–90 days and isolate your **top 10% of posts**. Look for the 1–2 things that appear in *all* of them, then put those into every future piece.
- Don't try to learn from your bottom 20%. Even if nothing is performing well, *something* is performing best, so study that.
- Re-run it monthly so you're always building on your newest best work.

### 6.4 The multiplier sheet: make performance comparable
- "It got 400k views" means nothing without a baseline.
- **Build a benchmark:** for each platform *and* format (Reels, carousels, statics), take the **median** (not the mean, because outliers skew it) of the last 75–90 days.
- **Report every post as a multiple of that benchmark:** 150k views against a 100k median = **1.5x**. The whole team speaks one language.
- **Wait about 11 days** after posting before scoring, because content often pops a week in.
- **Update the benchmark monthly** so the bar rises with your actual performance.
- Share sheets across platforms. A 3x on Instagram is a candidate to test on TikTok, and a "low" 50k views on TikTok might be a 5x there.
- For YouTube, tools already do this (One of 10, ViewStats). For short form you'll usually need a manual Google Sheet.
- **If a metric won't change your actions, don't track it.** Track 2–4 decision-driving metrics, not a massive dashboard.

### 6.5 Get objective feedback
- A video has about 20 variables, and you can't judge your own work without emotion.
- The MrBeast model: 3–4 friends at the same skill level critique each other's videos.
- The host's view: a YouTube coach is 100% worth it. He'd have paid 4x what he did.

### 6.6 The #1 short-form hack: study winners in order
1. **Your own winners.** Remake what already worked for you.
2. **The best people in your space** with as many bricks in common as possible. Remake their winners your way.
3. **A tool** (like Kane's Sandcastles) to surface winners if you don't have your own.

### 6.7 Fix your old content
- If your early podcast episodes are weak, don't live with them. **Delete the original, wait at least 3 days** (YouTube's copyright system "remembers" the video), trim it, and re-upload it with new packaging, for example as "from the vault."

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Post one at a time until about rep 50–100.
- [ ] Build a multiplier sheet per platform and format, and score each post at day 11.
- [ ] Hold a monthly top-10% review and write down 1–2 repeatable elements.
- [ ] Find 2–3 peers for weekly critique, or a coach.

## PHASE 7 — YouTube Long-Form: The Trust Engine

**The story:** Short form gets you discovered. YouTube builds trust fastest. It's a different sport with different rules: packaging is everything, and the reps are slow.

### 7.1 Why YouTube
- **Content-minute math:** about 10 blocks per rep versus half a block for a short.
- **More value per rep:** roughly 10 ideas per video instead of 1.
- **The X-factor:** viewers see your mannerisms and how you think, and they come to like you.
- **More rope:** 19 minutes in, a slow 90 seconds won't make people churn. On shorts they're gone in 2.5 seconds.
- **The Madden attribute idea:** everyone has a different spread of skill points. Short form disproportionately rewards creative editing. Long form rewards *explaining*. Play to your strengths.

### 7.2 What wins on YouTube (and will age well)
- **High-value, niche educational content with proof and credibility.**
- **Get the skill first.** If you have no skills, no audience and no offer, don't start by making content. Build a skill people will pay for, in traffic, funnels or offers. Kane's reasoning: *traffic is the most valuable*, because you can monetize traffic without an offer, but you can't monetize an offer without traffic.
- **Don't teach content until you've done content.** Learn it in another niche first. Kane used tech and AI.
- **"High value" means non-obvious and tactically implementable.** Say the things that are locked in your head, so a business owner can act on them and get the result.
- **The goal is one-to-one trust at scale, fast.** Higher value density builds trust faster.

### 7.3 YouTube is a packaging game
- If they don't click, the video might as well be a black screen. That's just math.
- MrBeast overinvests in packaging because without the click, the money spent on the video is wasted.
- **Short form versus long form:** the feed serves shorts automatically, and the hook *is* the package. On YouTube the viewer *chooses*, so you have to earn the click.
- **Packaging = idea + title + thumbnail + the intro that confirms the click.**

### 7.4 Kane's YouTube team from day one
- YouTube is a professional sport, so hire professionals from the start and fund them from other income.
- An ideas/strategy/packaging agency (under about $3k a month), a thumbnail designer, and an editor.
- **Kane only writes the script and records it.**
- Titles are done before recording. Thumbnails are made afterward by a pro in parallel with the edit. That only works because he's a pro. If you're not, do the thumbnail first.

### 7.5 The most common packaging mistake
- **Doing it last.** People spend 20 hours editing, burn out, and throw on a title.
- Do packaging first, while you're fresh, on the thing that matters most.
- Now that titles *and* thumbnails can be A/B tested, you have some flexibility. Still, know the core idea and keywords before you shoot.

### 7.6 The desire loop: how a viewer clicks
1. Scrolling, their **eye catches the thumbnail** and they lock in.
2. They **read the title**, looking for keywords that trigger a desire loop: **pain, FOMO, or desire.**
3. They **look back at the thumbnail**, which should *amplify* the title rather than repeat it. ("I solved the cheat code" beats "marketing conversation.")
4. They click, and the **intro confirms the expectation** within about 5 seconds.
5. They get the **first dopamine hit within 30–60 seconds.**
6. By then the title and thumbnail are forgotten. **The rocket-booster analogy:** the package gets the rocket up and then falls away.

### 7.7 Clickbait vs. bait-and-switch
- You *want* clickbait: bait the click, then *give* them the bait.
- Never bait and switch.
- If the best moment in a podcast happens at minute 45, **reorder the edit** so the payoff comes early and matches the package.

### 7.8 Hold attention through the whole video
- A good YouTube edit creates **tension, stakes and open loops** throughout.
- Don't give away all the value in the first 3 minutes. If you do, why would anyone stay for the next 10? *Sprinkle* the value across the video, because YouTube rewards watch time.

### 7.9 YouTube metrics
- **Business lens:** dollars per view. 5,000 views that make $50k is $10 per view. 300 views that make $300k is $1,000 per view. Make more of the second kind.
- **CTR** is a noisy metric.
- **The "1 of 10" ranking** (how a video ranks against your last 10):
  - **1–3 of 10:** leave it alone and study what worked.
  - **4–6 of 10:** usually a smaller idea TAM. Don't beat yourself up.
  - **7–10 of 10:** repackage it with a new title and thumbnail. That's the only lever you can change in real time.
- **Thumbnail testing:** you need a big gap (around 50%+) to call a winner. Once one pulls ahead, make it the winner and create **two new variants close to it** to test again.
- **New eyeballs vs. total views:** one video with 1M views a month can beat four videos with 250k each, even though the totals match. The 1M video reaches mostly *new* people. The 250k videos were 60–70% existing audience. That's why MrBeast posted every 4–8 weeks with huge effort per video.
- **Qualitative signal:** who reaches out. A text from a high-caliber person saying they got value from an episode doesn't show up in any stat.

### 7.10 Founder expectation mistakes
- **You're an entrepreneur using YouTube as free traffic, not a "YouTuber."** Treat it like paid ads: iterative, science-based experiments.
- Reps are slow (about 1 a week), so learning is slow.
- You need **on-target views**, not 100k. A couple thousand views can be huge.
- **Plan on 3–5 years.** A meaningful lead-gen machine in about 12 months is realistic. Fame isn't.

### 7.11 YouTube's compounding advantages
- **Evergreen:** videos and their description links live forever.
- **Every video is a 24/7 sales agent** that the algorithm can reactivate for free.
- Shorts have a **half-life of about 72 hours to 5 days**.
- **Repurposing:** turn long form into shorts, newsletter issues, carousels and posts (Phase 11.1).
- **Repackaging revives videos.** Some dead videos have exploded 9 months later after a new title and thumbnail.

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Write the title plus thumbnail concept *before* scripting.
- [ ] Name the desire loop (pain, FOMO or desire) in every title.
- [ ] Make sure the intro confirms the click within 5 seconds and delivers the first dopamine hit within 60, with open loops spread through the rest.
- [ ] Track "1 of 10" and repackage anything ranked 7–10.
- [ ] Track dollars per view or leads per view, not just views.

## PHASE 8 — Storytelling That Holds Attention and Converts

**The story:** Attention isn't the goal. Trust is. Storytelling holds attention, proof converts it, and educational content, done right, scales trust itself.

### 8.1 Why stories win
- They entertain, keep people hooked, change people's perspective, and create an emotional connection. People forget facts but remember stories.
- Even MrBeast, known for adrenaline, is leaning into storytelling (see *Beast Games*).

### 8.2 The five-part story framework (a guide, not a rulebook)
1. **Hook:** win attention immediately (Phase 5.2).
2. **Problem:** set the stakes with a specific, relatable conflict the viewer can see themselves in. Example: "I was working 14 hours a day for my hero, Gary Vaynerchuk, but I still felt stuck."
3. **Journey:** how you overcame it. **People resonate with progress, not perfection.** Show the messy middle: the losses, the doubts, "I don't know if this will work." (Caleb admits he doesn't know whether his expensive course will pay off.)
4. **Lesson:** the takeaway they can act on, broken into simple steps. That's what "providing value" means. Tie it back to your expertise and, ideally, your offer.
5. **Call to action:** a CTA doesn't have to be a sale. "Comment which tactic you'll use" drives engagement *and* makes viewers commit to action.

### 8.3 Finding the angle: the shock score
1. **Find the angle:** what's interesting? It's either *shocking* (entertainment) or *useful* (education).
2. **Ask:** what do most people believe that I disagree with?
3. **Shock score (0–100):** out of 100 people in a room, how many already believed this? The fewer, the more shocking.
4. The magic: say something **most people don't believe, but can believe quickly once you've said it.** That "aha" is contrast.
5. **Zigzag:** Prince Charming walks through the woods (expected: he keeps walking) → a dragon appears (shock) → they fight (expected, so give them that) → and so on.
- **Conflict** is a form of contrast: conflicting beliefs are contrasting beliefs.
- **The payoff** closes the last loop. No payoff feels like a bait and switch.

### 8.4 The five best story types
1. **Origin stories.** How you went from a Starbucks barista to the cover of Forbes. Where you are now isn't relatable, but the road there is. Treat every viewer as cold, and **never stop telling your origin story**, even when you're sick of it. (Caleb: reading *Crush It* at 15, which explains why he references Gary so much.)
2. **Failure stories.** People trust vulnerability more than success. Don't make it a sob story, though. Always include the lesson, so viewers skip your pain. (Caleb's cannabis-client pay cut.)
3. **Success stories.** Show before and after, so you aren't seen as someone who only fails. Example: growing Gary's TikTok from 300k to 3.5M in 3 months.
4. **Client success stories.** Proof that you can replicate results. Example: an agency owner who adopted "comment on what you *like* in an edit as much as what you want changed" texted a year later that it was the best team-training insight he'd ever heard. Editor output improved and client churn dropped.
5. **Industry stories (the contrarian take).** Name the industry's accepted wisdom and respectfully flip it: "Never tell your audience about your offer in organic content."

### 8.5 Conversion comes from proof
- The difference is in the *topic and substance*, not the mechanics. **Give them a taste of the result they'd get by buying.** For AG1, show the transformation. For an agency, demonstrate that you get the result.

### 8.6 The purpose of educational content: scaling trust
The chain:
1. You consistently pair yourself with **success stories**, so the audience associates you with success.
2. That creates **trust** in what you say.
3. Trust makes them act on your lessons, and acting gets them **results**.
4. Over time they stop associating you with *success stories* and start associating you with ***their* success**.
5. They learn that what they invest with you (time, money) always returns more. So when you make an offer, it feels cheap by comparison.
- Caleb's seminar story: buyers told the salesperson, "Shut up and take my credit card." They knew a $1,000 seminar would return at least $10k, and the free content had already delivered 50–100x.
- **Educational content is just scaling trust, and trust makes everything in business and life easier.**

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Map one story onto Hook → Problem → Journey → Lesson → CTA.
- [ ] For each script, list 3 claims with shock scores and lead with the highest one you can prove quickly.
- [ ] Plan your origin, failure, success and client-success stories, plus one industry-contrarian piece.
- [ ] Include one proof point or demonstration per video.

## PHASE 9 — Community: Turning Viewers Into Members

**The story:** Talking *at* people builds an audience. Talking *with* them builds a community that creates, defends and spreads your work.

### 9.1 From creator-first to community-first
- **Let them co-create.** A musician can post two versions of a verse and let fans vote. An author can do the same with chapter drafts or book titles. Fans become invested and turn into missionaries for the work.

### 9.2 Feedback loops: let the audience "read your mind"
- The best content ideas come from **DMs and comment questions**, not open-ended "what should I make?" polls. Audiences don't know what they want, but their questions reveal it.
- **Gary Vee's social listening:** when 5 people ask the same thing in 3 hours, thousands are feeling it. He answers immediately, without saying "so many people asked," and the comments say, "How did you know I needed this?"
- **Listen without changing your beliefs.** Use feedback to refine *how* you present ideas, not *what* you believe. Changing your beliefs to please the crowd makes you a politician.
- Example: a client hammering one intense message got pushback. They didn't change the belief. They added context about *why*, and the audience came around.
- **If you ignore the praise, you can ignore the hate.** If praise gets you high, hate will crush you. Tune out the trolls and mine the genuine comments.

### 9.3 Make your audience the hero
- Share community wins, transformations and case studies publicly, such as results from your free weekly Zoom call, or a recorded call with a follower who got a result.
- These won't be your top performers, but they encourage more people to act and send you their wins.
- **UGC:** prompt followers to post their own takes on your lessons, then stitch or feature them. This creates a perpetual engagement cycle, and even a follower with 300 followers is promoting you.

### 9.4 The three pillars of community content
1. **Educational:** solve their biggest problems.
2. **Conversational:** make them feel heard and involved.
3. **Social proof:** real stories of your impact.

### 9.5 Engagement mechanics
- **Make engagement the goal, not just views.** Ask for a fire emoji, or post a counter-industry take and invite debate: "I'll be in the comments going back and forth."
- **Script in a debatable point** to create comment "cyclones" (Kane).
- **Respond to comments.** If the 3 people commenting now get no reply, why would more start? Replies signal to newcomers that commenting is worth it.
  - Ideally *you* respond, even if only 5 comments a week. When you can't, use a clearly labeled **team account** (for example, "Team Gary Vee" replied while Gary was on flights). Never pretend to be you.
- **Super shares and super likes:** a share from a large account (or, anecdotally, a like from Instagram head Adam Mosseri) seems to be weighted far more heavily.
- **Inside jokes and shared language:** strong communities have lingo outsiders don't get, like gym bros' "bulking season" or Harley riders' Sportster jokes. Use your niche's existing language to show you're an insider, or invent your own.

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Every week, list the top 3 repeated questions from DMs and comments, and answer one in content.
- [ ] Post one co-creation vote this month.
- [ ] Reply personally to comments within 24 hours.
- [ ] Define 1–2 pieces of shared language for your community.

## PHASE 10 — Monetization: Trust Before Transactions

**The story:** Rented attention on the platforms has to be turned into owned relationships, and then into revenue, without breaking the spell or burning trust.

### 10.1 Trust is the currency that comes before the transaction
- Most people monetize too early, push offers before trust exists, and wonder why nobody buys. (Caleb admits even he was tempted to charge for his course.)
- If bills demand it, do what you have to do. But the best case is that before monetizing, your brand has:
  1. **given massive value up front,**
  2. **proven credibility and expertise,** becoming the go-to authority, and
  3. **built a relationship.** When people feel they know you, sales feel effortless, with no manufactured scarcity or urgency.

### 10.2 Pick a monetization model that fits your strengths and your audience's needs
1. **Services:** agency, consulting, coaching. High touch, for deep expertise people already ask you about.
2. **Products:** digital (ebooks, templates, playbooks, courses, very high margin, so start here) or physical (books, apparel).
3. **Affiliates and sponsorships:** only promote products you actually use. Pairing with junk makes your audience associate you with junk.
4. **Community and memberships:** recurring revenue through paid groups, masterminds and paid newsletters. This is a home for superfans.
5. **Ads and content monetization:** YouTube AdSense, podcast or newsletter ads. Pair this with another model, because it usually just covers production costs early on.
- **Not sure where to start?** Read your email, DMs and comments. Your audience is already telling you what to sell.

### 10.3 Share the knowledge, sell the execution
- Giving more away creates more demand. It doesn't reduce it.
- Caleb's Harley exhaust story: he watched about 100 free YouTube videos on swapping the pipe, realized it was too complex and too expensive to risk on his bike, and paid the Harley dealership (which made those videos) to do it.
- Give away the framework, and sell the help, templates and done-for-you execution.

### 10.4 Build your offer stack (start with one offer, refine it, then expand)
1. **Free content:** trust and awareness
2. **Lead magnet:** a mini-course, SOP, checklist or playbook to capture email or phone number, so you aren't at the mercy of algorithms
3. **Low ticket ($10–100):** an ebook, mini-course or template that lowers the barrier to entry
4. **Mid ticket ($500–5,000):** group coaching, community membership, deep courses
5. **High ticket ($10,000+):** 1:1 consulting, agency services, done-for-you work
- Most brands don't lead with high ticket. They earn it.
- Kane's fast track to $1M profit (Phase 14) is the exception that proves the rule: high-ticket done-for-you or done-with-you, fueled by very high-value YouTube.

### 10.5 Lead magnets: the rails of every funnel
A lead magnet trades free value for an email address. The types:
1. **One-off PDF or doc:** "Here's my storytelling framework as a doc, link below."
2. **3–5 day email series:** e.g. *viralityblueprint.com*
3. **Free community:** e.g. *Wavy World*, with an email collected at entry
4. **Waitlist landing or sales page**
5. **Form:** e.g. "Want our editors? Fill out the form."
6. **An irregular "only when it's great" newsletter** (Caleb's version): no fixed cadence, sent only when something is truly valuable.
- **Why email:** it's the last place not fully run by algorithms. Open rates are high (the host sees about 60%). People trust email and buy from it.
- **The simple 7–8 figure model:** content → lead magnet → email → drip or automated sequence → ask them to buy the thing that solves their problem.

### 10.6 Let your content do the selling
1. Teach a valuable lesson and end with a *soft* CTA.
2. Share client success stories.
3. Document your process from behind the scenes.
4. Address the unspoken objections.
5. Use lead magnets to capture data.
- **Show the result instead of pitching the product.** Weak: "Buy my ebook on personal branding." Strong: "I helped John go from 0 to 10k followers in 4.5 months using this exact strategy. The full system is in my ebook, link in bio."
- The advanced version: don't pitch in organic at all, and **retarget** engaged viewers with paid ads.

### 10.7 Native embeds vs. non-native ads (the hypnosis principle)
- Good content *hypnotizes* the viewer into forgetting they're watching (white hat only): click confirmed → dopamine hit 1 → dopamine hit 2 → hypnotic flow.
- **A non-native ad** ("We'll be right back… buy this couch") breaks the spell.
- **A native embed** weaves the pitch into the conversation, or sets it up so your product is the answer. For example: mention Ganon, ask "do you use ManyChat?", talk about it for 4 minutes, and that's the ad.
- **Placement math:** put *your own* embeds early and often. Put interrupting sponsor ads *after* the average view duration. People who stay to the end are stickier and convert well.
- Ask for the subscribe. The host notes about 83% of listeners aren't subscribed.

### 10.8 Brand deals and repurposed assets
- Brands price deals on your last 10 videos (average views), engagement, and followers.
- Your content can do double duty: brands may want to use your video on their own channels, as ads, or through whitelisting.

### 10.9 Play the long game
- **Deliver so much value that people feel guilty not buying.** Give away your real frameworks, not a watered-down teaser. You want people thinking, "If this is free, what are they charging for?"
- **Build return behavior:** recurring formats (challenges, weekly breakdowns) and bingeable playlists grouped by topic. Think Netflix series, not one-hit viral videos.
- **Protect your reputation at all costs.** Rebuilding trust is far harder than keeping it. Before every monetization move, ask: **"Will this make my audience trust me more or less?"** If less, it's not worth it.
- Short-term cash grabs feed you today and starve you tomorrow.

**✅ Action checklist**
- [ ] Pick one monetization model and one first offer.
- [ ] Build one lead magnet that matches that offer.
- [ ] Write a 5-email drip that ends with the offer.
- [ ] Put 1–2 native embeds in every long-form video, placed early.
- [ ] Run the "trust up or down?" test on every monetization decision.

## PHASE 11 — Scale: Building the Content Machine

**The story:** Once you can make great reps, you multiply them. You repurpose intelligently, go platform-native, systematize the workflow, and keep experimenting.

### 11.1 Pillar content and the waterfall
- **Pillar content** is long form: a YouTube video, a deep thread, a long LinkedIn post, a newsletter.
- **Mine it for gold:** self-contained paragraphs, punchy quotes, a 5–6 sentence summary, and contrarian or nuanced moments to clip.
- **The waterfall model** (credit to Gary Vee, around 2015–16 with Daily Vee): one YouTube video becomes shorts, quote posts for Threads and X, a LinkedIn post rewritten from the transcript, the same post on Facebook, the LinkedIn text as an Instagram caption, and carousels. That's easily 10+ pieces from one shoot.
- **Carousel rules:**
  - Make roughly 2 slides per video section, each with one simple image and 1–2 sentences.
  - **Put a hook on slides 1 *and* 2.** Instagram re-serves unengaged carousels starting on slide 2, which gives you a second chance.
  - Listicles convert best to carousels. For deep, complex videos, expand just one section into a carousel.

### 11.2 Repurposing 2.0: contextual, not copy-paste
- In 2025 the platforms are fragmented. Clipping works as a start (and for your first ~3 years), but the best content is **native to each platform**.
- **Use one platform's winners to seed the others.** Caleb's case study: a client's tweet blew up (social listening), so they
  1. made a short using the tweet as the hook plus the backstory,
  2. printed the quote as a poster and posted a photo of it (her top Instagram post ever),
  3. expanded it into a LinkedIn post with the same hook (a top-3 post),
  4. and turned it into a 25–30 minute YouTube video (in her top 10).
  - The same message, repackaged for each platform.
- **The "Sarah" analogy:** you are you at Saturday brunch with family, at cocktails with friends, and at Monday's business meeting. The topics, tone and vocabulary change, but you're still authentic. Platforms are just different rooms.

### 11.3 The short-form workflow loop and Kane's team
**Idea → Research → Scripting → Recording → Editing → Posting → Analyze.** Repeat.
- Editing is a bounded task and easy to outsource.
- **Ideas are a bottomless pit.** That's where the real time savings are, and it's the hardest part to hand off.
- **2 dedicated editors** (full-time, from an agency in the Philippines). Each makes about 1 video every 1–1.5 days.
  - On off days they build templates and systems so the other editor can move faster.
  - They have **virtual credit cards** to sign up for any AI tool that speeds up visuals.
- **Content operator / "ideas guy"** (an EA-plus, offshore):
  - Scans **idea pipes**: other creators, X/Twitter, Reddit, YouTube, newsletters. The right pipes differ by niche.
  - Shortlists and **develops** each idea (visuals, angle, examples), with one Google Doc tab per idea.
  - Runs **pitch calls** once or twice a week, like a Hollywood showrunner. Kane says yes or no.
  - It took **3–6 months to clone Kane's brain** into him. Now he's as good at finding ideas as Kane.
- **A Gen AI specialist ("the rabbit"):** plays with new tools all day. You need "a rabbit for each hole."
- **A manager** (Rugo) for brand deals and email.
- Kane still writes scripts himself in about 20 minutes, then shoots and hands off.

### 11.4 Make it sustainable
- **Play long term.** If you hate video, don't force it yet. Content should be sustainable for *years*.
- **Build a simple machine:** set a baseline (for example, 1 YouTube video every two weeks and 3 shorts a week), hold it for 2–3 months, then raise the bar.
- **Remove yourself from execution** as soon as you can afford it, starting with freelancers or agencies to prove ROI.
- **Build a filming environment for the talent** (it's the *team's* job, not the talent's):
  - The videographer **nods**, and nods harder as the content gets better, to give real-time feedback.
  - **Fist bumps and daps** between takes, naming 1–2 specific good moments.
  - The advanced version: the crew listens **as a skeptic** and fills objection gaps after each section.
  - **Message the talent 15–20 minutes after the shoot** with specific, *honest* praise ("Impressive how you pushed through X"). This pairs film days with good feelings, so the talent looks forward to them.
- **Know your energy rhythm.** Film at your peak hours (for Caleb, mornings, with a dip at midday).
- **Protect content time like a board meeting.** If you keep rescheduling it, the team concludes it isn't a priority.

### 11.5 Platform-native team assignments (the 85/15 system)
- The ceiling-breaker: give the *same raw footage* to different editors who each cut it natively for their platform. Hooks often change, and the middle often stays the same.
- **Assign platforms to people by strength:** best editor → platform #1 plus #4, second best → #2 plus #5, third → #3 plus #6. Each spends **85%** on their primary and **15%** experimenting on their secondary.
- Example: Angela owns Instagram (about 7 native pieces a week) and TikTok (1–2 a week).
- When Gary gave Caleb TikTok (at 300k followers), Caleb's first week was just **8 hours a day of scrolling and note-taking**. It grew to 3.5M.
- **Why it works:** focus (no "vanilla" content), ownership tied to each creator's own data, better retention, a reason for fans to follow you on every platform (different content in each place), and it trains editors into strategists and future leaders.
- On an already massive team getting billions of impressions a year, this delivered a **1.5x** lift. At the top, that's like adding 5% to a 700 lb squat.
- **Matching people to platforms:** TikTok needs someone fluent in current humor and trends. LinkedIn needs someone who understands your subject deeply. YouTube needs someone who *lives* on YouTube and notices shifts like the 2024–25 vlog comeback (and how modern vlogs differ from 2015's).
- **Drop the "social media manager."** The platform owners post and manage their own channels.

### 11.6 Data culture
- Make data the team's decision-making framework: about 90% data-backed ideas, 10% free creative.
- Hire for data-mindedness, and ideally bring in a **data nerd** whose job includes analysis and sharing insights. One such hire transformed Caleb's team.
- **Brick by brick:** keep an internal list of the "bricks" that reliably improve outcomes. It becomes your onboarding playbook.

### 11.7 Build in experimentation
- Teams plateau from **complacency**: they learn what works and stop trying new things, while the algorithm keeps changing. True innovation often has no data behind it.
- **Content hackathons (monthly or quarterly):**
  - 9:00 kickoff (15–30 minutes) with one rule: "I don't care how it performs. Make something you've never made."
  - Everyone makes 1–2 wild pieces. Lunch is paid for.
  - Each person uploads to a shared slide deck by 4:00. At 4:30, each presents their *why* in a 60–90 minute call.
  - The team votes on the most innovative piece. The winner gets published and a prize.
  - Some of Caleb's longest-running top formats came from hackathons.
- **The 70/20/10 framework:** 70% proven formats, 20% iterations on winners (new hooks, CTAs, formats), 10% pure experiments. Most teams get stuck at 70%, and comfort doesn't build iconic brands.

### 11.8 AI tools in the stack
- **Sandcastles.ai** (Kane's tool) handles the content workflow. Its AI scripting is good for beginners (level 1–6), not yet for 9–10 writers.
- **LLMs:** Gemini (Gemini 3 is excellent) and Claude are Kane's favorites. ChatGPT is a distant third and too agreeable.
- **Higgsfield:** doing to AI video what Instagram filters did to photography. It templatizes creation.
- **Freepik Spaces:** a node-based workflow. Input one script line and get 5–10 on-brand AI B-roll clips.
- **What AI is good and bad at:** great at *compressing and combining*, poor at *generating novel ideas*.
- **Quality over speed:** Kane won't take an hour to do worse what he can do well in 10 minutes.

- [ ] Take one proven winner and remake it natively for a second platform.
- [ ] Assign each creator a primary and secondary platform (85/15).
- [ ] Schedule your first content hackathon, and set your 70/20/10 split.
- [ ] Write your film-day environment rules (nods, daps, the post-shoot message).

## PHASE 12 — Team: Hire, Onboard, Develop, Retain

**The story:** Your team isn't just there to execute. They multiply your vision. Bad hires cost months and six figures, so hire deliberately, onboard properly, and grow people until they don't want to leave.

### 12.1 Hire for constraints, not "roles you're supposed to have"
- Hire only for **needs**, not wants. **Hire to solve problems, not to fill roles.** Find the bottleneck:
  - Footage piling up unedited → **editor**.
  - Editors idle with nothing to cut → **videographer or content strategist**.
- **Three questions:**
  1. What's pulling people away from high-value work? (A strategist spending 50% of their time editing needs an editor.)
  2. What's slowing execution?
  3. What opportunities are you missing for lack of resources? (For example, you want 2 YouTube videos a month but can only produce 1, so find the constrained step.)
- Hire to create **leverage**, not to offload busywork. Five editors without enough footage is just cost.

### 12.2 Hire platform specialists, not generalists
- A great Netflix editor isn't a great YouTube editor. A documentary DP isn't a vlog shooter. A great brochure designer can be a bad YouTube thumbnail designer, because thumbnail best practice breaks traditional design rules.
- **A generalist "social media manager" who's great at every platform is great at none.** Hire channel-specific people. Early on, have each creative *emphasize* one platform.
- If LinkedIn drives your business, **hire a LinkedIn writer before a video editor.** (Caleb's firm started on LinkedIn with a freelance LinkedIn writer.)
- A typical sequence: YouTube editor (2x output) → short-form editor (repurposing for 2–5x impressions) → platform strategist.

### 12.3 Define the role clearly
- Spell out every requirement, including the unusual ones (for example, "we only film 4–8 a.m. or 8 p.m.–midnight").
- Ask: Is this role creating, managing, or analyzing? Single-platform or multi-platform (at this stage)? How does it ladder up to the bigger picture?

### 12.4 Write job descriptions with the 4 Rs
1. **Role:** the contribution and function. (Example: a "Brand Director" to build and scale the CEO's personal brand across YouTube and LinkedIn.)
2. **Responsibilities:** what they own daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly, in tactical detail.
3. **Requirements:** skills and experience (for example, 3+ years growing a personal brand, filming and editing both long and short form, managing creatives, storytelling).
4. **Results:** the measurable outputs (for example, a minimum of 2 YouTube videos a month, and 2 written plus 2 video LinkedIn posts a week).
- Plus **communication cadence** (for example, "respond on Slack by 7:30 a.m.") and **core values**, which you want candidates to already live by.
- Vague job descriptions attract vague candidates.

### 12.5 The hiring funnel (don't shortcut it; every bad hire came from rushing)
1. **CV plus an optional video.** The CV reveals patterns (10 jobs in 5 years is worth asking about). The video reveals personality and hunger. One candidate sent a multicam video about why he fit, and he got hired.
2. **Screening call:** salary, in-person or remote, communication and travel expectations, plus 2–3 light technical questions. These also catch candidates reading live AI-generated answers. Caleb hit this 5 times in one month.
3. **Technical interview:** tests strategic knowledge *and* execution. Don't ask "Can you copy our style?" Ask "How would you innovate a new style?" You're judging how they think.
4. **Technical assessment:** mirrors the real job. For example, 72 hours to create a new short-form style from 1 hour of raw footage. This tests time management, delivery, communication and execution, with bonus points for showing their process. **Give every candidate the same assessment.**
5. **Culture interview:** use real scenarios. "Someone critiques you directly in front of 5 people, what do you do?" "A non-manager demands you drop your work, what do you do?" "The CEO communicates tersely and doesn't want small talk, are you OK with that?"
6. **Final interview:** with the most senior person they'll interact with. Close every open question. Their questions matter too: no questions is a red flag, and strategic questions are a great sign.
- You're looking for about 80% fit, and deciding whether the other 20% is trainable or unneeded.
- **Small team?** Combine steps (screening plus technical, then the assessment, then culture plus final), or borrow an agency partner to run the technical interview.
- **Why it works:** it filters out mass applicants (especially AI-automated ones), checks platform fit, checks culture fit, and speeds up hiring.

### 12.6 The tryout funnel (for editors, designers and strategists)
- Monday: announce tryouts, with registration open until Thursday noon. Thursday evening: email the raw footage and the brief (a 12–20 minute YouTube edit, a thumbnail and title, and a short). Due Monday 9 a.m.
- About 1,500 registered and about 220–250 submitted. Everyone edited *the same footage*, so comparing them was easy. Then the normal funnel followed (screening → technical → culture → final).
- How they prioritized when they couldn't finish everything is itself a signal.
- It only works for roles where one source file can go to everyone (not videographers).

### 12.7 Hire for culture and train for skills
- Technical skill can be trained fast. Hunger, empathy and adaptability can't.
- **Trevor:** about a 6/10 editor and a 10/10 on culture (humble, eager, hardworking). Trained up.
- **Jason Leva:** an average tryout edit, but a sponge who thanked Caleb for blunt feedback. One of Caleb's best hires ever.
- Hire for mindset and train for execution. A motivated hire grows into the role, and a disengaged one stays stagnant.

### 12.8 Platform red flags when hiring
- **TikTok/Instagram:** you want someone who lives on the platform and understands hooks and open loops. Red flag: someone obsessed with production value. Remember, the audience defines quality.
- **LinkedIn:** you want a writer of concise, high-value, conversation-starting posts. Red flag: stiff, corporate "elevator pitch" writing. Human voices stand out.
- **YouTube:** you want someone who knows the first 15–30 seconds are everything and knows how to hold tension across the video. Red flag: a traditional-production purist on a run-and-gun vlog (for example, someone who insists on prime lenses and perfect lighting every scene).

### 12.9 Start lean and grow intentionally
- A small, nimble, SEAL Team Six–style crew beats a bloated team (and avoids painful layoffs).
- Early on, **specialists who are willing to wear multiple hats.** For example, one person who films *and* edits. Decide which of the two skills matters more.
- **Why lean:** you avoid payroll before content ROI is proven, stay able to pivot (speedboat vs. oil tanker), and everyone understands every role (better communication, empathy for turnaround times, backup when someone's out).
- **Expand gradually:** start with multi-role hires → document every workflow → specialize as volume grows. Staying in flow saves the 30–45 minutes it takes to get back into flow after each interruption.

### 12.10 Full-time vs. contractors and agencies
| Factor | Full-time employee | Contractor / agency |
|---|---|---|
| **Budget** | Higher long-term cost (salary, benefits, training) | Cost-effective for projects and specialist needs (Caleb's motion designer is a contractor) |
| **Workload** | Daily, ongoing work | Project-based, flexible, temporary (e.g., event coverage, filming twice a month) |
| **Expertise** | Grows with you, so you invest in training | Should arrive as the expert, "batteries included." Hire for 3–6 months, get their playbooks, and treat it as a fast pass |
| **Speed** | 60–90 day ramp, but continuity | Immediate, but agencies churn staff (often 7 editors in a year), which hurts consistency |
| **Brand consistency** | Absorbs nuance through internal meetings | Needs the most oversight (for example, flashing the wrong "successful entrepreneur" on screen) |
| **Scalability** | Grows into leaders who carry the culture | Great for testing a hypothesis (for example, 3–4 months of LinkedIn) before committing |

**The three growth stages:**
1. **Year 0–1:** mostly contractors and agencies. Stay lean, test platforms, and have the founder shape the content.
2. **Years 1–3:** bring critical roles in-house (for example, an in-house editor, with the agency reduced to ideation, scripting, packaging and consulting).
3. **Years 3+:** bring all high-volume critical roles in-house for speed and communication. Keep agencies for niche specialties, platform relationships (for example, a media buyer's Meta rep), and sprint capacity, so you never have to hire and fire for a launch.

### 12.11 Onboarding: the 30-60-90 plan
- Caleb's two experiences: one job gave him no laptop, no tools and no expectations (anxiety). Another sent a 30-60-90 plan five days before he started (motivation).
- **Days 1–30, orientation and core learning:** the vision, values, workflows, tools (content calendar, Asana or ClickUp), how communication actually works, small tasks for early wins, and frequent check-ins. Editors shadow a senior editor, review top-performing videos, and do internal rough cuts.
- **Days 31–60, ownership:** they execute their core responsibilities independently, build their own workflow, and get feedback on process and output (for example, editing full YouTube videos and reviewing their performance).
- **Days 61–90, full integration:** they own the most important responsibility with minimal supervision, and results show up against KPIs (for example, watch time at or above average, or at least improving). **Then hold a 90-day review.** Praise specific wins, and correct any behavior now. Letting it slide after this point is on you.
- **Other onboarding pieces:**
  1. Send a clear plan before day one (handbook, vision deck, strategy deck, 30-60-90).
  2. Assign an onboarding buddy.
  3. Schedule regular check-ins (for example, three 10-minute check-ins a week in month one: "What was most confusing? What roadblock can I bulldoze?"). Celebrate new hires who speak up. Caleb high-fived a day-one hire for contributing in a meeting.
  4. Build a resource hub (optional, and a Google Drive folder is fine): SOPs, playbooks, tutorials, a founder Q&A.
- **Keep roles clear after onboarding:** update role descriptions as they evolve. Overlapping ownership means nobody does the task (for example, a community manager and editors both "analyzing metrics"). Set KPIs per person.
- **Payoff:** faster ramp-up, stronger retention (losing an employee costs about 2.5x their cost), and more ownership.

### 12.12 Develop and retain
- **A culture of ownership, not a "checklist army":** SOPs and checklists matter, but when someone brings you a problem, ask "How would you handle it if I got hit by a bus?" Push them to bring solutions, not problems, and let them fail on things that aren't mission-critical. Caleb's creative director stopped texting him for every on-set decision once Caleb stopped solving them.
- **Teaching is the ultimate hack:** have team members lead the weekly trainings. It builds mastery (explaining *why* you cut on a 12/36-frame cadence), reveals hidden passions (sound design, storytelling), and makes people the go-to experts for each topic.
- **Mentorship loops:** pair senior and junior people by skill, both ways. For example, a new but more technical videographer teaches tech, and the veteran teaches how the organization works.
- **Align work with career goals.** In one-on-ones, ask: What's your long-term goal? What comes after this job? What skills do you want to build? (For example, an editor who loves motion graphics gets to animate the lower thirds. A manager who wants to be a CEO gets director-level responsibilities.) Great people leave when they see themselves growing *away* from where they want to go.
- **Go deep before wide (specialization):** specialists execute better, need fewer revisions, and earn more over their career. Trevor's crossroads: switch to long form at the bottom of the ladder, or stay in short form as the go-to expert. He stayed, and became senior editor and then lead editor managing three editors at 22–23. He's broadening now, which is the accordion applied to a career.
- **Pay for strategic education** (for example, a YouTube retention workshop from someone who worked with MrBeast). Send 1–2 people, and have them teach it back to the team.
- **Run effective one-on-ones:** workload and blockers ("What can I bulldoze?"), then long-term goals ("What have you wanted to learn but haven't had time for?"). A TikTok strategist who wanted to be a creative director was given multi-platform campaigns to develop and pitch.
- **Summary:** push ownership down, make leading trainings part of their job, tie their growth to company growth, go deep before wide, and hold structured check-ins. If you don't make time for feedback, you'll end up making time for hiring.

### 12.13 Build a strong culture on purpose
- **Core values** guide hiring, decisions and daily behavior. Hire for alignment, publicly reward value-driven behavior (Slack shoutouts, awards), and **lead by example**. A youth pastor once told Caleb: "Whatever you do, they'll do times 10." If the team is off, look at yourself first.
  - Patagonia's example: it hires for sustainability passion, gives grants to employees' environmental initiatives, gives 1% of sales to the planet, and closes on powder days.
- **Radical transparency:** explain the *why* behind decisions, like canceling an editor's 6-day, 13-hour-a-day project because the talent disliked it (honoring that preference builds the talent's trust and vulnerability). **Own your mistakes first and loudly.** Announce pivots early, never at the last minute.
- **Care about the person:** notice what they love (a Lego fan gets the Millennium Falcon set). When someone's sick, send soup and juice instead of doubting them. If they were faking, they'll feel guilty, so you win either way.
- **Inspire loyalty through growth:** show how each person's work drives company growth, check in on their career goals, and give **stretch opportunities**. Trust begets trust.
- **The maker/manager schedule** (Paul Graham): meetings are a manager's job and a maker's enemy. One interruption costs a maker 30–60 minutes of flow.
  - Caleb's schedule: makers 7:00–12:00, meetings 12:00–5:00.
  - Monday is a full meeting day. Tuesday and Thursday–Friday are maker mornings with manager afternoons. **Wednesday is a full maker day.**

### 12.14 Remote vs. hybrid vs. in-person
| Factor | Remote | In-person | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flexibility | High (can justify slightly lower pay, parents value it) | Low | Medium |
| Collaboration | Needs strong tools, a clear communication SOP and time-zone planning, with few happy accidents | Easiest: live feedback beats Frame.io notes | Balanced |
| Productivity | Depends on the individual (about half thrive at home) | Depends on the individual (chatty people lose focus, and open floor plans mean constant shoulder taps) | Tailor days per person |
| Cost | Lowest (no office, no relocation) | Highest (rent, utilities, perks) | Worst of both (you pay for empty desks) |
| Talent pool | The whole world, including 10/10 talent outside LA/NYC prices | Local, or pay relocation premiums (LA → El Paso) | Better, because partial remote makes relocation easier |
| Culture | Hardest, so be intentional: daily Slack check-ins, virtual happy hours, quarterly in-person meetups mixing work and fun | Easiest, though young post-COVID hires may need coaching on conduct and conflict | Best of both |
- Caleb's verdict: **hybrid is best if you can afford it.** In-person favors the company, and remote favors the employee. Rank these factors for your situation and decide from that.
- If you can't choose, borrow the other model's benefits: in person, weekly team breakfasts. Remote, Thursday virtual happy hours.
- **Final team principles:** culture, alignment, growth and trust; reinforce values daily (branding works internally too); radical transparency (transparency begets transparency); make growth a priority; and **lead with empathy, not fear**, so creativity flourishes.

- [ ] Write the job description with the 4 Rs, plus communication cadence and core values.
- [ ] Set up the 6-step hiring funnel (or the combined 3-step version).
- [ ] Write a 30-60-90 plan before the new hire's first day.
- [ ] Put maker and manager blocks on the calendar.

## PHASE 13 — The Future of Content & Attention

**The story:** A 20-year window of free distribution is closing. Here's what's coming and how to position for it.

### 13.1 The AI content tsunami
- The more commoditized the content, the sooner AI replaces it.
- **The replacement spectrum, from easiest to hardest to replicate:** text (X/Twitter is already flooded) → short form → long form → **3-hour uncut live streams**, which will be the last to go.
- **The AI soft-cancellation paradox:** everyone wants to use AI to go faster, but getting caught using it gets you "soft-cancelled."
- The media game has maybe 2–3 years left.

### 13.2 How to build a moat
- **Go niche, own a term fast, and go signature and one-of-one:** unique experiences, credibility, proof and stories that a beginner can't copy. This is Caleb's "your story is your secret weapon," applied as a moat.
- **Build a brand people search for.** When the algorithm changes, a trusted name gets searched for, and a views-only account vanishes.
- **Build an AI-proof skill:** psychology and attention. Humans are the buyers until agents are buying from agents.
- **Every company is a marketing company and a content company.**
- Dan Koe's four future-proof skills: **marketing, sales, writing, speaking.**

### 13.3 Future platforms
- **A zag toward human-only content:** an "Etsy" of verified-human content (biometric or blockchain).
- **AR glasses** will reinvent content formats, followed later by VR and 3D.
- Incumbents won't be caught flat-footed again the way Instagram was by TikTok.
- **Sora 2** faces backlash against "AI slop," but might evolve into on-demand, personalized, Netflix-style content.
- **Platform risk is real** (the TikTok blackout), which is one more reason to be on 2–3 platforms and to own email.

### 13.4 The window is closing
- Short form has about 2 years before AI content is indistinguishable and floods feeds. Long form has about 5.
- **About 2008 to 2028: roughly 20 years of free distribution at scale. It's never coming back.** The For You feed era (about 2020–2028) is about 8 years.
- **After 2028:** likely pay-to-play, where platforms charge (say) $10 a post to rise above the AI baseline.
- **Go all in for the next 3 years.**
